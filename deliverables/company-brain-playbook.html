If you only say one thing

There are two completely different markets here, and almost everyone confuses them. The big, established players — Notion, Guru, Glean, Atlan — put big corporate logos on their websites. The new startups put other tiny startups on theirs.

That means a young company cannot win by selling to big corporations. It wins by selling to other small, fast-moving AI companies — the ones building AI assistants of their own, who feel the pain today and can decide to buy in a week instead of a year.

How to use this page. Each play below has four parts: what it means in plain words, who is actually doing it, the evidence we found, and a sentence you can say out loud. Read the top four properly — those are the ones worth talking about. The rest are useful if the conversation goes deeper.

The ten plays, ranked by value

Ranked by how much they matter for a company at our stage: how much new business the move brings in, set against how hard it is to pull off.

  1. 01RANK

    Sell to the startups sitting next to you

    Impact · Very high Effort · Low Works immediately
    What it means

    Don’t start by chasing big companies. Start by selling to the startups you already have access to — the ones in your accelerator batch, your investor’s portfolio, your group chats. They trust you, they reply to your messages, and they can say yes without a procurement process.

    Who is doing it

    Hyperspell is the clearest example. Nearly every customer on its website is another small AI startup — AgentMail, Hobbes, Scale Agentic, Corgi, Eragon, Smallest, FinalRoundAI. Not one large corporation. Falconer and Glen do the same thing.

    The evidence Of the 37 customers we researched properly, almost all are seed-stage AI companies of roughly 10–200 people. The huge corporate names only ever appear on the websites of the old, established tools — never on the websites of the new startups.
    Say it like this

    “I looked at who these companies actually put on their websites. The new ones sell almost entirely to other small AI startups. So the fastest route to revenue isn’t enterprise sales — it’s the network we’re already inside.”

  2. 02RANK

    Solve one painful job, and name the tool you’re replacing

    Impact · Very high Effort · Medium Biggest differentiator
    What it means

    “We’re a company brain” is not something anyone has a budget for. But “your engineering docs are out of date and it’s costing your team hours every week — rip out Confluence” is a problem someone will pay to fix today. Pick one narrow, annoying, obvious job. Then name the specific product you’re replacing, so the buyer instantly understands what changes.

    Who is doing it

    Falconer does this best. It sells one thing — engineering documentation that drifts out of sync with the actual code — and it openly positions against Confluence and Notion. Compare that with Glen, which calls itself an “institutional learning layer.” Nobody wakes up wanting to buy an institutional learning layer.

    The evidence Falconer alone accounts for 6 of our 37 researched customers and 4 of the 18 testimonials that carry a real person’s name and job title — and its case studies are the only ones that state the customer’s industry, location and company size. Glen has a similar number of customers but its quotes are vague, like “It just works and works well.” That tells you nothing about what problem was solved.
    Say it like this

    “The companies with the strongest customer proof are the ones selling a specific, boring, painful job — not a vision. Falconer sells ‘your docs are stale, replace Confluence’ and gets detailed case studies. The ones selling a ‘learning layer’ get vague one-liners, because there’s no budget line for a learning layer.”

  3. 03RANK

    Put a real name, face and job title on every testimonial

    Impact · High Effort · Very low Cheapest win here
    What it means

    A logo on a website proves almost nothing — anyone can add a logo. A named person, with their job title, saying what specifically got better, is worth ten logos. It is also the single cheapest thing on this whole list: you already have the customers, you just have to ask them.

    Who is doing it

    Hyperspell quotes Adi Singh, Founder of AgentMail: “one shared source of truth across our whole team and agents has let us automate most of our internal operations.” Mem0 quotes Abhi Arya of OpenNote: “Mem0 turned our AI tutors into true learning companions — tracking each student’s struggles, strengths, and learning style.” Both name the person and the result.

    The evidence 25 of the 91 companies we studied show no customers at all on their websites — including well-funded ones. Only 18 testimonials across the whole market carry a real name and title. It is a wide-open gap, and it costs nothing to close.
    Say it like this

    “A quarter of this market shows no customer proof at all, and only eighteen testimonials in the entire category have a real name and job title attached. That’s the cheapest possible advantage — we just have to ask our users for a sentence about what actually changed.”

  4. 04RANK

    Give the code away free, charge for the hosted version

    Impact · High Effort · High Slow, then compounding
    What it means

    Publish your core software free and open on GitHub so any developer can download and try it without talking to sales. Most will run it themselves — that’s fine, they become your marketing. The ones who don’t want the hassle of running it pay you to host it for them. You get reach first and revenue second.

    Who is doing it

    This is the standard playbook for the technical half of the market: Mem0, Zep, Letta, Supermemory, Cognee, Honcho, Papr and Basic Memory all do it.

    The evidence 24 of the 91 companies lead with an open-source product. It works, but note the trade-off: it takes a long time, and it mostly wins developers rather than teams. It suits a company selling building blocks to engineers, not a finished product a whole team logs into.
    Say it like this

    “Roughly a quarter of the market is open source — Mem0, Zep, Letta, Supermemory. It buys real reach with developers, but it’s slow and it wins individual engineers rather than teams. It’s the right move if you’re selling a building block, not a finished product.”

  5. 05RANK

    Go after teams who already built this themselves

    Impact · High Effort · Low Underused
    What it means

    The best customer isn’t someone who’s never heard of the problem. It’s someone who hit the problem, built their own patchy solution, and is now quietly sick of maintaining it. They don’t need convincing that the problem is real — they’ve already paid for it in engineering time. You just have to show up.

    Who is doing it

    Almost nobody is targeting this deliberately, which is exactly why it’s an opening. But the proof that these buyers exist is in the data: Adapta, Flow and Scira all built their own memory systems and then switched to a vendor.

    The evidence About 8 of the 37 researched customers had already built memory in-house — roughly one in five. That cuts both ways: it’s a ready-made list of warm prospects, but it also means a fifth of any target list may decide to build rather than buy. Worth naming that risk out loud.
    Say it like this

    “About one in five of these customers had already built their own memory system before buying one. That’s our warmest possible audience — they’ve proven they want it and they’re tired of maintaining it. It’s also our biggest risk, and I’d rather we name it than pretend it isn’t there.”

  6. 06RANK

    Sell memory your customer can resell to their own users

    Impact · High Effort · Medium Changes the price
    What it means

    There’s a big difference between selling a tool a company uses internally and selling a piece they build into the product they sell. Internal tools are a cost — buyers push the price down. But if your memory becomes a feature their customers pay for, it turns into part of how they make money. People pay far more for that, and they don’t churn easily.

    Who is doing it

    Only Hyperspell and Cognee show real evidence of winning this. Yet the demand is obvious in the data — Hobbes, Luccid, SlideSpeak and Adapta all need memory that is separated per end-customer.

    The evidence Four of the researched customers need this and only two vendors visibly serve it. That’s a gap between what buyers need and what the market currently offers.
    Say it like this

    “The most valuable version of this isn’t a tool a company uses internally — it’s memory they build into the product they sell. That stops being a cost line and starts being part of their revenue, which completely changes what they’ll pay. Four of the customers I looked at need it and only two vendors serve it.”

  7. 07RANK

    Plug into other people’s AI tools instead of asking people to visit yours

    Impact · Medium Effort · Low Fast distribution
    What it means

    There is now a common standard — MCP — that lets any AI assistant connect to outside tools. If you support it, your product shows up inside whatever AI tool someone already uses, instead of being another website they have to remember to open. You borrow someone else’s daily habit rather than trying to build a new one.

    Who is doing it

    Memory Store makes it the whole pitch — any AI assistant that supports the standard can connect straight to your company’s memory. Mem0 shipped a dedicated version called OpenMemory. Supermemory, Falconer, Sentra and Wato all do it too.

    The evidence 14 of the 91 companies now lead with this. It is quick to build and gets you in front of people cheaply — but because it’s quick for everyone, it won’t stay a differentiator for long. Treat it as table stakes, not a strategy.
    Say it like this

    “Fourteen of these companies now ship an MCP connector, so their product appears inside whatever AI tool the customer already uses. It’s cheap distribution and we should absolutely have it — but it’s becoming table stakes, so I wouldn’t build the positioning on it.”

  8. 08RANK

    Aim at developers using AI coding tools

    Impact · Medium Effort · Medium Filling up fast
    What it means

    Developers using AI coding assistants hit the memory problem harder than anyone: the assistant forgets the earlier decisions, so they explain the same context again every session. It’s a sharp, daily, obvious pain — which makes it easy to sell into.

    Who is doing it

    Glen, Mosaic, ByteRover, Blume, Sylph and Falconer are all aimed here.

    The evidence 10 of the 91 target this specific group. The pain is genuine, but the lane is crowding quickly — it’s a good beachhead, not a long-term moat.
    Say it like this

    “Ten companies are chasing developers who use AI coding tools, because that group re-explains the same context every day. Real pain, easy to sell — but it’s crowding fast, so I’d treat it as a way in rather than the whole plan.”

  9. 09RANK

    Write “alternatives to [competitor]” pages

    Impact · Medium Effort · Low Slow burn
    What it means

    When someone is unhappy with a tool, they search for alternatives to it by name. If you’ve written the page that answers that search, you meet a buyer at the exact moment they’ve decided to switch. It costs one afternoon per page.

    Who is doing it

    Naboo runs a dedicated alternatives page. Comparison articles built around Hyperspell, Mem0 and Zep already rank in search — which is, in fact, how we found several of these companies during this research.

    The evidence These pages were a genuine discovery route in our own research. That’s direct proof they get read by people actively comparing options.
    Say it like this

    “Comparison pages are how I found half these companies myself. Someone unhappy with a tool searches its name plus ‘alternatives’ — if we own that page, we meet them at the exact moment they’ve decided to switch. It’s an afternoon of work per page.”

  10. 10RANK

    Sell safety and control to regulated industries

    Impact · Low now Effort · High Later, not now
    What it means

    Banks, hospitals and pharmaceutical companies will not let company data leave their own systems. If you can promise the data stays inside their walls, and show exactly who saw what and when, you can charge a lot. But these deals take months and need trust a young company hasn’t built yet.

    Who is doing it

    Saphenia targets financial services and pharma directly. Naboo offers to run inside the customer’s own systems. Sentra, Coworker and Zep lean on audit trails and tracking what changed.

    The evidence Only about 11 of the 91 compete on this, so it is genuinely uncrowded. It ranks last purely because of timing — the sales cycles are long and it suits a later, better-resourced stage.
    Say it like this

    “Almost nobody competes on security and audit trails — only about eleven of the ninety-one. It’s real money and it’s wide open, but the sales cycles are long, so I’d file it as a year-two move rather than something to chase now.”

Four mistakes worth pointing out

Naming what competitors get wrong shows sharper thinking than listing what they get right. These four are all visible in the data.

Becoming the tenth identical “connect your Slack and Notion” tool

At least nine companies — Gyld, Agently, Pensieve, Almanac, Lore, Compendium, Memory Store, Hyper and Company Brain — describe themselves in near-identical words. Between them they show almost no named customers. Sounding the same as everyone else is the most expensive mistake in this market.

Launching with nothing to show

25 of 91 companies display no customer, no case study and no quote. Some are well funded. A visitor has no reason to believe any of it works, so they leave.

Describing the technology instead of the problem

Phrases like “institutional learning layer” or “persistent context graph” describe how the thing is built, not what goes wrong without it. Nobody has a budget for a layer. They have a budget for “our support team keeps answering the same question.”

Building memory for one person instead of a team

Personal memory — remembering what you told an assistant — is crowded and being given away free by the big AI platforms. Shared memory across a whole team is the harder problem, has far fewer credible competitors, and is the thing a company will actually pay for.

Words you’ll need

Short, plain definitions for the terms that come up in this conversation.

Company brain
A shared memory for a whole company — everything decided in Slack, docs and meetings, kept in one place so both people and AI assistants can look it up.
Agent
An AI assistant that does tasks on its own, not just answers questions. Answering support tickets, for example.
MCP
A shared standard that lets any AI assistant connect to outside tools. Like USB — one plug that fits many devices.
Seed stage
A young company that has raised its first proper money. Usually 10–50 people. Decisions are quick and often made by the founder.
YC / Y Combinator
A well-known startup programme. Its companies form a tight network that buys from each other — which is why play 01 works so well.
Open source
Giving your code away free so anyone can use it, then charging for the convenient hosted version.
Logo wall
The row of customer logos on a website. Weak proof on its own — a named quote is worth far more.
Design partner
An early customer who helps shape the product, usually cheap or free, in exchange for close attention and being named publicly.

Where this came from. We mapped 91 companies across five parallel searches — the Y Combinator directory, general web search, open-source project listings, funding announcements and comparison articles. We then read each company’s own website, customer pages, blog and launch posts, and collected 403 customers they publicly advertise. 37 of those customers were researched in depth. Every number and quote on this page comes from something published by the companies themselves.

One caveat worth saying out loud. This only sees what companies choose to publish. A company with no logos on its site may still have paying customers it hasn’t named — silence is a hint, not a verdict.
